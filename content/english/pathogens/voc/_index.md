## Background

- **Pathogen** : _SARS CoV 2_ belongs to the coronavirus family. Because of its RNA genome and replication mechanism, it is prone to accumulating mutations. Many _SARS-CoV-2_ have circulated over time.

- **Transmission**: Transmission is primarily via respiratory droplets and aerosols when infected individuals breathe, talk, cough, or sneeze. Indirect transmission via contaminated surfaces (fomites) is possible but generally less significant. The virus can spread from symptomatic, presymptomatic, or asymptomatic individuals.

- **Sources and risk factors**: Typical sources include ready‑to‑eat foods, refrigerated foods with long shelf‑life, smoked fish, soft cheeses, vacuum‑packed cold‑cuts, and other chilled foods where _L. monocytogenes_ can multiply slowly. High risk groups for serious illness typically include the elderly, immunocompromised individuals, and pregnant people (with both mother and fetus at risk).

- **Seasonality**: The risk of infection is higher when encountering infected individuals in close proximity, poorly ventilated indoor spaces, or crowded settings. Some variants exhibit increased transmissibility or allow immune escape, both of which can increase the risk of infection. Individuals with waning immunity or suboptimal vaccine responses (e.g. in immunocompromised individuals) are also at greater risk of infection. The risk of serious illness is higher in individuals lacking prior immunity (through vaccination or previous infection), though it is possible for otherwise healthy individuals to become seriously ill.

## Brief Summary of the Outbreak

_SARS-CoV-2_ emerged in humans in late 2019, but existed in animals prior to this. The first cases of the novel coronavirus (nCoV) were identified in China in December 2019, and the virus quickly spread worldwide. In response, [the World Health Organization (WHO) declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) on 30 January 2020](https://www.who.int/europe/emergencies/situations/covid-19), and later recognized the outbreak as a pandemic on 11 March 2020.

Since then, many variants infecting humans have appeared. The variants are monitored by health agencies, who use multiple tools for tracking, sharing, and processing data (e.g. [Nextstrain](https://nextstrain.org/), [GISAID](https://gisaid.org/)). In May 2021, [WHO adopted the Greek alphabet nomenclature](https://www.who.int/en/activities/tracking-SARS-CoV-2-variants/) (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Omicron, etc.) for naming variants. Variants were categorised as [Variants of Concern (VOC), Variants of Interest (VOI), and Variants under Monitoring (VUM) depending on evidence of impact (transmissibility, severity, immunity evasion, detection)](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/sites/default/files/documents/threat-assessment-covid-19-emergence-sars-cov-2-variant-omicron-december-2021.pdf). For example, variants were categorised as _VOCs_ if there was clear evidence that they would have a marked negative impact on the population. Multiple variants were classified as VOCs, including Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron variants.

On 5 May 2023, more than three years later, [the WHO Emergency Committee on COVID-19 advised that the situation no longer met the criteria for a PHEIC](https://www.who.int/news/item/05-05-2023-statement-on-the-fifteenth-meeting-of-the-international-health-regulations-\(2005\)-emergency-committee-regarding-the-coronavirus-disease-\(covid-19\)-pandemic). The WHO Director-General accepted this recommendation, marking the end of COVID-19's classification as a global health emergency. While this shift does not signify the end of the pandemic itself, it indicates that the acute international crisis phase has concluded. A review committee will now develop ongoing recommendations to support countries in managing COVID-19 as an established, long-term health challenge.

In 2023, WHO refined variant classification definitions. VOC status became reserved for variants posing new, added risk. After this point, sublineages of Omicron were more often classified as VOIs or VUMs.Since then,  multiple newer variants (e.g. JN.1, NB.1.8.1, XFG, LP.8.1, etc.) have been tracked, with risk assessments and monitoring by the [World Health Organization (WHO)](https://data.who.int/dashboards/covid19/variants/) and the [Europe Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC)](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/covid-19/variants-concern/).

## Outbreak Timeline

| **YEAR** | **DATE**  | **OBSERVATION**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| :------- | :--------: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 2019     | December | SARS CoV 2 was first detected in humans in Wuhan, China.                                                                                                          |
| 2020     | September | [Alpha Variant (20I/501Y.V1, lineage B.1.1.7) was first identified in the UK and later spread to 114 countries](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abg3055)                                                                                                                  |
| 2020     | October | [Beta variant (B.1.351) was first identified in South Africa](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03402-9).                                                                                     |
| 2020     | December  | [Gamma variant (P.1) was first identified in Brazil (initially detected in travellers)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abh2644).                       |
| 2021     | February  | [Delta variant (B.1.617.2) was first detected in India (it later became globally dominant)](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa2108891).           |
| 2021     | November  | Omicron variant (B.1.1.529) was first detected in South Africa / Botswana. [WHO designated it as a VOC on 26 November 2021](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/sites/default/files/documents/threat-assessment-covid-19-emergence-sars-cov-2-variant-omicron-december-2021.pdf).          |
| 2022     | Early 2022 (Jan)  | Omicron became dominant globally. Subvariant BA.1 peaked and was soon replaced by BA.2 in many regions.                |
| 2022     | February  | [Subvariant BA.2 overtook BA.1 as the dominant variant in many regions](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05031). BA.2 was estimated to be more transmissible and better at immune escape than BA.1 (in modeling).                |
| 2022     | Mid / Late 2022  | Omicron sublineages such as BA.4, BA.5 increased in prevalence followed by emergence of further descendants (e.g. BQ.1, BQ.1.1) in late 2022.                 |
| 2023    | Overall  | Continued circulation and diversification of the Omicron variant led to recombinants and subvariants (XBB, etc.). WHO shifted classification practices (VOC was reserved for new threats, subvariants were handled as VOIs/VUMs). [ECDC released documentation regards the these variant changes](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/sites/default/files/documents/231201%20variants%20changelog.pdf).               |
| 2023    | July -August  | [BA.2.86 (and derivative JN.1) was first detected in Germany. JN.1 was later designated as VOI.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10818819/)        |
| 2024     | Jan–Mid 2024 | While JN.1 was circulating, the other emerging lineages were monitored (VOIs/VUMs).                |
| 2024     | March   | [The WHO launched CoViNet](https://www.who.int/groups/who-coronavirus-network), a global network for coronavirus variant surveillance and coordination.            |
| 2024     | Late 2024  | [New lineage designations and risk evaluations were published. (e.g. JN.1, LP.8.1, etc.)](https://data.who.int/dashboards/covid19/variants)      |
| 2025     | Early 2025 | Detection and monitoring of sublineages such as NB.1.8.1 and XFG were in progress.                |
| 2025     | June    | XFG was categorised by WHO as a variant under monitoring (VUM) and NB.1.8.1 was also under evaluation.          |
| 2025     | Mid 2025 onwards  | Variant competition continues, new mutations and subvariants are monitored as pandemic evolves.     |

## Data Visualisation

- [This Nextstrain build](https://nextstrain.org/groups/neherlab/ncov/sweden), maintained by the Neher lab and Nextstrain team, focuses specifically on sequences from Sweden.
- [ECDC created a dashboard](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/covid-19/situation-updates/variants-dashboard) that shows a variants of SARS-CoV-2 in EU/EEA.
- [Data from ECDC on SARS-CoV-2 variants in the EU/EEA](https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/publications-data/data-virus-variants-covid-19-eueea) contains information about the volume of COVID-19 sequencing, the number and percentage distribution of variants of concern (VOC) by week and country.
- The [Swedish Pathogens Portal offers interactive dashboards on SARS‑CoV‑2](https://www.pathogens.se/dashboards/), including data on wastewater quantification, variant dynamics, serology testing, and vaccine coverage. It also visualises whole genome sequencing data and highlights trends in variant growth.
